01 / The big idea
Start here

Sell the fixed bike.
Not the tools.

Imagine your bike is broken. Two people offer to help.

Person one

“Pay me to use my tools for two hours.”

Person two

“I’ll fix the chain, check the brakes, and test the bike so it’s ready to ride.”

The second explanation makes the deal easier to understand. You know what the person will do and what you will get.

Explain your agency offer the same way.

A service = the work

“I design websites.”

This names a skill, but leaves the details open.

An offer = the whole deal

“A five-page website.”

For cleaning businesses, with a working quote-request form, a defined timeline, and a clear price.

So, what does “high-ticket” mean?

In this guide, it means a premium-priced package that represents a meaningful investment for your buyer.

There is no magic price that makes an offer good. A big price does not automatically mean big value.

Remember this Do not start with “How much can I charge?”
Start with “What useful change can I deliver?”

02 / Build the offer
Keep it simple

Five blocks.
One clear offer.

Each block answers a question your customer needs answered.

1

Who do you help?

Choose a specific kind of customer—not “everyone.”

“Local cleaning businesses with outdated websites.”

2

What problem do they have?

Use words the customer would actually say.

“People have to call us just to ask for a cleaning quote.”

3

What will be better?

Describe the finish line. Separate what you deliver from results you cannot fully control.

“Visitors can request a quote from their phone.”

4

What is the plan?

Explain the work in a few simple steps.

“Plan the website → Build the pages → Test and launch.”

5

What is the deal?

Explain the price, timing, what is included, what is excluded, and what the customer must provide.

“Five pages. Two revision rounds. One agreed price.”

The clarity test Your buyer should not have to guess
what they are paying for.

03 / See it in action
A complete example

The Cleaning
Website Makeover

Fictional teaching example. The price and timeline are illustrative—not market benchmarks.

“We rebuild your website so visitors can understand your services and request a quote from their phone.”

Who it is for

Established local cleaning businesses whose websites make requesting a quote difficult.

Delivery timeline

Launch within 21 days after required materials and account access arrive, provided approvals stay on schedule.

01 · Plan

Agree on the pages, message, and information needed.

02 · Build

Create the pages and connect the quote-request form.

03 · Launch

Test the website and show the owner how to use it.

What is included

Five pages, clear service descriptions, one quote-request form, mobile testing, two revision rounds, and a 30-minute handover.

What the customer provides

Business details, photos they may legally use, account access, and feedback within two business days.

Example investment US$3,000

US$1,500 to begin.
US$1,500 when the agreed website is ready to launch.

Not included: Advertising, ongoing traffic generation, extra pages, and third-party software fees.

After-launch support: Errors in the delivered pages and form reported within 14 days of launch are fixed at no extra charge. New features are separate work.

The boundary: This promises a functioning website and quote form—not a guaranteed number of leads or sales.

04 / Choose the price
Make the numbers make sense

A big number
is not a strategy.

Choose a price you can explain—and work you can deliver sustainably.

1. What will it cost you to deliver?

Count your time, people helping you, tools, revisions, and support. Your own working time is not free.

$3,000 Customer pays
− $1,200 Estimated delivery costs
= $1,800 Remaining before other costs

Imaginary example in US dollars. The remaining $1,800 is not automatically take-home profit. Overhead, taxes, and other costs may still need to be covered.

2. How important is the problem?

Ask what happens when it stays unfixed. Does it waste time, make work harder, or frustrate their customers?

Use the buyer’s real situation—not numbers you invented to make your price look small.

Ask: “What would make fixing this worthwhile?”

3. Does the package make sense to the buyer?

Learn which alternatives they are considering and what they expect from a provider. Covering your costs does not automatically mean the buyer will accept the price.

Need a lower-priced option?

Consider a smaller package instead of the same work at a much lower price. For example: three website pages and one revision round instead of five pages and two rounds.

A sensible price needs to work for both sides.

05 / Build trust
Less doubt. More clarity.

Help the buyer feel
safe saying yes.

A bigger promise is not the same as better proof.

1

Show something real.

Show relevant work and explain what you actually delivered. Do not turn one success into a guarantee for everyone.

Starting from zero? Create a sample or offer a small, clearly scoped paid pilot.

“Demonstration project—not a client result.”

2

Define the support.

Explain what is covered, what conditions apply, and what happens when something goes wrong.

Avoid vague promises such as “unlimited changes until you are happy.”

“Your package includes two revision rounds.”

3

Add a useful extra.

A bonus should help the customer use the main service. It does not need to be a giant pile of unrelated material.

Website bonus: a ready-to-use email template for replying to quote requests.

4

Use honest deadlines.

Mention limited availability only when it is true. Do not invent “last spots,” countdowns, or deadlines to pressure a buyer.

No real deadline? Leave the countdown out.

Keep this boundary: Do not invent testimonials, clients, or results. Promise work you can responsibly deliver—not sales outcomes you cannot control.

Show real work. Explain the limits. Keep your promises.

07 / Check and test
Before you sell it

Can someone understand
the deal without guessing?

Check each box only when your draft makes the answer clear.

The right person can recognize themselves.

I have named a specific customer—not “everyone.”

The problem sounds like something they would say.

I have removed confusing business words.

The buyer knows what they will receive.

The deliverables and finish line are clear.

The plan is easy to follow.

I can explain it in a few simple steps.

The price and responsibilities are visible.

Payment terms, extra costs, and customer tasks are explained.

The promises match my abilities and evidence.

I am not presenting hoped-for results as guarantees.

The next step is obvious.

There is one clear action to take.

Now show it to potential customers.

Start with three people who fit your audience. Treat this as a learning exercise—not proof that the whole market will buy.

“What do you think you would get from this offer?”
“Which part feels useful, unclear, or unnecessary?”
“What would you need to know before considering it?”

Listen before explaining. Write down their exact words. Use what you learn to improve the offer.

Your first offer is a draft to test—not a promise of instant sales.
